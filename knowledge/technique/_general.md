# Technique — general knowledge

Shared content the individual exercise cards point to, rather than each
repeating it: contested-cue evidence notes, warm-up protocols, breathing
and bracing mechanics, and the self-reported-complaint → response map.
Condensed from the project's research notes on technique cues.

---

## 1. Evidence notes on contested cues

For each topic: what's well supported, what's coaching folklore or
overclaimed, and how to phrase it to the athlete.

**Knees over toes.** The old blanket rule ("never let your knees pass
your toes") is not well supported as a universal safety rule. Forward
knee travel is a normal, load-bearing part of a full-depth squat for
most people; restricting it artificially just relocates the moment to
the hips and lower back rather than eliminating stress. `[sourced:
Nuckols/Stronger by Science, echoing the broader S&C literature]` Knee
travel scales with torso angle, stance width and limb proportions; the
relevant marker is pain and whether tracking is grossly uncontrolled
(collapsing inward), not a toe-line rule. **Phrasing:** "there's no
fixed rule against the knee passing the toes — pay attention to whether
it's comfortable and whether the knee tracks in the same direction as
the toes, not to a toe-line rule."

**Neutral spine dogma.** "Never let your back round, ever" is stated far
more absolutely in gym folklore than the evidence supports. Injury-rate
data in strength sports shows low-back injuries occur in both
flexion-avoidant and flexion-tolerant populations. `[sourced: reviews
cited in Stronger by Science's deadlift guide]` This is not a green
light for indiscriminate heavy rounding: a braced spine moving in a
controlled way through a modest range is different from one collapsing
uncontrollably under a load too heavy for that day's control.
**Phrasing:** "keeping the back from rounding is a reasonable general
target, especially under heavy load, but a small, controlled amount of
movement isn't automatically dangerous — sudden or uncontrolled rounding
is the thing worth paying more attention to."

**"Chest up."** A genuinely useful cue for many lifters as a proxy for
thoracic extension, but a cue, not a biomechanical law — some lifters
(e.g. very long-femured squatters) will never look "chest up" the way a
shorter-femured lifter does, and forcing it can push them into a worse
position. **Phrasing:** "'chest up' is one useful cue among several for
keeping the upper back tight — if it doesn't feel right for a given
body, an equally valid alternative is 'try to bend the bar across your
shoulders,' aiming at the same upper-back tightness a different way."

**Valsalva and blood pressure.** A brief Valsalva maneuver is essentially
unavoidable at high effort and produces a real, measured, transient
spike in blood pressure and intra-thoracic/intra-abdominal pressure.
`[sourced: Hackett & Chow 2013, J Strength Cond Res — review; MacDougall
et al. 1985 — direct intra-arterial measurement during heavy resistance
exercise]` In healthy people without cardiovascular disease this
transient spike is not itself dangerous and is part of how the technique
stabilizes the spine. It is a real contraindication conversation for
uncontrolled hypertension, certain cardiac conditions (aortic
aneurysm/dissection history, recent cardiac or eye surgery, some
arrhythmias), or anyone a doctor has specifically told not to
breath-hold under strain. **Phrasing:** "holding your breath briefly
during a hard rep (bracing) is a normal, well-studied part of lifting
technique and safe for most healthy lifters — but if there's a history
of high blood pressure that isn't well controlled, or a heart or eye
condition a doctor has flagged, that's worth a conversation with them
before relying on hard breath-holds, and I'd rather point to that than
guess."

**Butt wink.** Some posterior pelvic tilt/lumbar flexion at the very
bottom of a deep squat is close to universal even in well-coached
lifters, and spinal flexion under load does increase shear stress
relative to a neutral spine at the same load. What's overclaimed: that
any visible wink is inherently and immediately dangerous, or always
mobility-fixable — mobility, anatomy (hip socket depth/orientation) and
simple depth all interact. **Phrasing:** "some rounding right at the
very bottom of a deep squat is extremely common and not automatically
dangerous, but working depth is reasonably set at the point just before
that rounding shows up for that person, rather than forcing depth past
it."

**Lifting belts.** A belt measurably increases intra-abdominal pressure
compared to bracing without one, increasing trunk rigidity, with modest
performance benefits on squat/deadlift-type lifts mostly evident near
maximal loads. `[sourced: replicated finding underlying Stronger by
Science's "Belt Bible"]` What's overclaimed: that a belt is mandatory, or
primarily an "injury prevention device" — the performance/stiffness case
is much better supported than a hard injury-prevention claim.
**Phrasing:** "a belt reliably increases the pressure you can brace
against, which tends to help on heavier squat/deadlift-type sets — it's
a reasonable tool for top sets, but it isn't required, and keeping some
beltless training in the mix is a common recommendation so the bracing
skill doesn't depend entirely on the belt."

**Knee sleeves/wraps.** Different tools, not interchangeable. Wraps
store real elastic energy through knee flexion and can measurably
increase how much can be squatted (commonly cited at roughly 10%+ with
practiced use), shifting some load from knee extensors toward hip
extensors. Sleeves are a much milder intervention — mostly warmth, a
small amount of compressive support and proprioceptive feedback; the
performance effect is smaller and more subjective. **Phrasing:** "wraps
and sleeves aren't the same thing — wraps can meaningfully change how a
heavy squat feels and moves and take practice to use well; sleeves are
more about warmth and comfort, with a smaller and more person-dependent
effect on the lift itself."

**Deadlift bar path.** The bar (more precisely, the body-plus-bar
center of mass) needs to stay balanced over roughly mid-foot throughout
the pull, and should travel close to the legs rather than drifting
forward. What's sometimes overclaimed: that the bar must travel in a
perfectly straight vertical line — some forward drift at lighter
relative loads is a natural consequence of body-mass distribution, not
automatically a flaw. **Phrasing:** "the bar staying close to the legs
the whole way up is the practical target — a perfectly straight vertical
line is an approximation that mostly holds at heavier loads, not an
exact rule that applies the same way to every set."

**Shoulder position in bench press.** Retracting (and usually
depressing) the shoulder blades before and during the descent is
associated with a shorter, more mechanically favorable range of motion
and is widely taught as protective for the shoulder joint. The exact
amount of scapular depression versus neutral versus slight elevation is
more person-dependent than sometimes presented. **Phrasing:** "pulling
the shoulder blades together and generally down before the bar comes
down is a well-supported starting point for shoulder comfort — the exact
amount of depression is worth adjusting to whatever feels most stable
and pain-free for that person."

**Hip thrust mechanics.** A controlled EMG study found meaningfully
greater gluteus maximus activation in the hip thrust than the back
squat, especially near lockout, while the squat produced greater
quadriceps activation. `[sourced: Contreras et al. 2015, J Appl
Biomech]` What's overclaimed: treating higher EMG amplitude as directly
equivalent to a proportionally larger hypertrophy outcome — EMG is a
reasonable proxy for which muscle is doing more work in that instant,
not a precise predictor of long-term growth. **Phrasing:** "hip thrusts
do produce noticeably more glute activation than squats in the studies
that have measured it directly, especially at the top of the movement —
that's good evidence it's an effective glute-focused exercise, though
it's not a guarantee of exactly how much extra muscle that translates to
over months of training."

## 2. Warm-up protocols

**General warm-up (before the working sets of a session).** A short
(roughly 5–10 minutes) warm-up raising heart rate and body temperature
slightly is a reasonable default before any main lift — light cardio
(bike, rower, brisk walk) or a handful of easy bodyweight movements
(bodyweight squats, arm circles, band pull-aparts) covering the joints
that will be loaded that day. Its purpose is moving from "cold" to
"ready to load," not being a training stimulus in itself.

**Ramp-up sets (specific warm-up before a working weight).** Scale the
number and size of jumps to how heavy the working weight is — heavier
top sets need more ramp-up steps, lighter accessory work needs almost
none.

Example ladder for a 100 kg × 5 working set (a moderately heavy day). The
percentages are the rule and the kilograms only illustrate them — a 225 lb
working set ramps through the same percentages, rounded to what the bar can
actually be loaded to:

| Set | Load | Reps | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Empty bar (~20 kg) | 8–10 | Groove/mobility, not effort |
| 2 | ~40 kg (40%) | 5 | Still easy |
| 3 | ~55 kg (55%) | 3 | Starting to feel the weight |
| 4 | ~70 kg (70%) | 2 | Getting closer to working effort |
| 5 | ~82 kg (82%) | 1–2 | Near-working-weight, low reps |
| 6 | ~92 kg (92%) | 1 | Last warm-up single |
| — | 100 kg | 5×working | First working set |

**General scaling rule of thumb:** smaller jumps and more steps as the
weight gets closer to the working set (avoid one huge jump right before
a heavy top set); reps drop as load rises. For a lighter working set or
accessory/isolation work, 1–2 light ramp-up sets (or none) is typically
enough — a heavy ladder is a "big compound lift, heavy day" tool, not
something every exercise in a session needs. The main barbell lifts
(squat, bench, deadlift, overhead press) benefit most from a deliberate
multi-step ramp; accessory/isolation work after the main lift typically
needs far less, since the body is already warm.

**Static stretching before lifting.** Holding static stretches for a
prolonged period (most reliably shown for holds of roughly 60 seconds or
longer per muscle group) immediately before a strength/power effort
tends to produce a small, temporary reduction in maximal force/power
output for the stretched muscle, fading within roughly 10–15 minutes.
This is a reasonable argument against a long, static-stretch-heavy
routine immediately before a heavy top set — not strong evidence against
static stretching in general, brief static holds, or stretching at other
times (after training, on off days). Dynamic warm-ups (moving through a
range of motion) are the more commonly recommended default immediately
before lifting specifically because they don't carry this same
temporary strength-reduction effect.

## 3. Breathing and bracing

**The technique, in plain terms:** a deep breath low into the belly (a
diaphragmatic breath — the stomach/obliques expand, not just the chest),
then brace the torso as if bracing to take a punch to the stomach (with
a belt, more like trying to expand the torso to press outward against
it). Hold that breath and brace through the sticking point, and release
near or just after the rep is essentially completed. This breath-hold
under tension is the Valsalva maneuver in a lifting context.

**When it's appropriate:** for anything approaching a genuinely heavy
effort on a compound, multi-joint lift (squat, deadlift and its
variants, overhead press, bent-over row) — where the lift actually
challenges the lifter's control, not just casual warm-up-weight reps.
For lighter accessory/isolation work, continuous, controlled breathing
(exhaling on the effort portion) is a perfectly reasonable default.

**On multi-rep sets:** reset the breath between reps — at the top of a
squat or deadlift, or with the bar racked/resting between reps of a
press — rather than holding one breath through an entire set of 5+
reps. Trying to hold a single breath through many hard reps in a row is
a common source of the "ran out of air, felt dizzy" complaint.

**Contraindications, named plainly:** uncontrolled or poorly managed
high blood pressure, a history of aortic aneurysm or dissection, certain
arrhythmias, recent eye surgery or a retinal condition a doctor has
flagged, and any other case where a doctor has specifically advised
against breath-holding under strain. For any of these: don't improvise a
workaround — say plainly this is a conversation for their doctor, and in
the meantime lighter loads with continuous breathing (exhaling through
the effort) is a safer default than guessing.

**Belt usage:** a belt is a reasonable tool specifically on the heavier
sets of squat, deadlift and overhead-press-type lifts. Not necessary on
every set of every session — using it on top sets while keeping some
beltless work in the program keeps the underlying bracing skill
developed rather than fully outsourced to the belt. A belt should sit
snugly around the waist (roughly at navel level), tight enough to brace
against, not so tight it's uncomfortable to breathe into.

## 4. Self-reported problem → response map

| Athlete says | How to respond, within limits |
|---|---|
| "I've hit a plateau on my [lift] lockout" | Ask where exactly the sticking point is (off the floor/chest vs. mid-range vs. near lockout) and how it's felt across the last few sessions (grinding harder each week vs. suddenly stuck). Name the general programming levers that exist (a lockout-biased accessory, a volume or intensity change, a deload if fatigue looks high) as questions for `/coach:review`, which decides them from the logged history — not as a call made here, and never as one guaranteed fix. |
| "My shoulder clicks" | Clarify: pain with the click, or painless? A painless click with no other symptoms is common and often not concerning, but never diagnose it either way. Pain, weakness, or a new/worsening click → recommend a physiotherapist or doctor rather than guessing at a cause. |
| "My lower back pumps/burns on deadlifts" | A very common, usually benign sensation (erectors working hard, a normal training pump) as long as there's no sharp pain, numbness, or pain radiating down a leg. Ask specifically to rule those red flags out. |
| "I can't feel my lats working" | A very common mind-muscle-connection issue, not unique to this athlete. Offer the standard cues (shoulder-blades-down initiation, a light-resistance engagement drill before the working set, "pull the bar into your body") and suggest a lighter load specifically to practice the sensation. |
| "My knees hurt on squats" | Pain goes to the pain protocol, not to a cue: check the athlete's words against `knowledge/safety.md` §6's Red list first — any Red marker → recommend a physiotherapist or doctor and leave the movement to the traffic light (`safety.md` §1), with no troubleshooting by cue. With no Red marker, ask: front, side, or behind the knee? During the descent, at the bottom, or driving up? Only on heavy sets or every rep? Anything changed recently (volume, depth, footwear, a new exercise)? Then offer attention points only — e.g. whether the knee tracks in the same direction as the toes (§1, "Knees over toes") — and leave any change to load, depth or range to the pain protocol and the planner. |
| "Is my form good?" | Cannot be answered directly without seeing the lift — see the technique agent's phrasing rules for exactly how to redirect this. |
| "My wrist hurts on [pressing exercise]" | Ask about hand/grip position (bent back under load, or stacked over the forearm) and whether wraps have been tried. Suggest the stacked-wrist cue and/or wraps first; persistent pain despite that is a "get it checked" flag. |
| "I feel dizzy/light-headed during heavy sets" | Ask whether breath is held for the whole set versus reset between reps, and whether this is new. Explain the normal breath-held blood-pressure response as context, but also ask about any history of high blood pressure or heart/eye conditions — any of those present → recommend a doctor conversation rather than continuing to troubleshoot via cues. |
| "I get pain that shoots down my leg" | A clear red flag regardless of exercise — recommend seeing a doctor or physiotherapist promptly rather than offering a technique cue, and suggest pausing the exercise that provoked it until assessed. |
| "My form feels different/off today but nothing hurts" | Normalize this — day-to-day variation from fatigue, sleep, stress and ordinary session-to-session noise is completely normal, not itself a sign of a problem. Offer to talk through what felt different if the athlete wants to troubleshoot it. |

**General rule for this table:** a complaint that could be pain is
checked against `safety.md` §6's Red list before this table is used at
all — any Red marker goes to a professional and the pain protocol, not to
a row here. Past that check, troubleshoot comfortably within "here's a
common cause and a cue to try" for anything that sounds like ordinary
technique/fatigue/unfamiliarity, as attention points only — a change to
the programme's load, volume or range is never this table's to make. Move to "this is
worth a professional's eyes" for pain that radiates, a new or worsening
symptom, or a red-flag combination (e.g. dizziness plus a known
cardiovascular condition). When in doubt between the two,
recommending an in-person check that turns out unnecessary beats
continuing to troubleshoot something that genuinely can't be seen from
here.
